Where Did You Fall?

Let's map your decisions onto the same framework economists have used for 45 years to explain why humans aren't rational — and why that's completely predictable.

The Value Function

In 1979, Kahneman and Tversky proposed something radical: people don't evaluate outcomes in absolute terms. They evaluate them as gains or losses relative to a reference point — and the two sides of that curve are deeply unequal.

The right side — gains — rises steeply, then flattens. The left — losses — drops far more sharply. This asymmetry is the engine behind nearly every bias you have.

Kahneman & Tversky, Econometrica (1979)

Losses feel roughly 2× more painful than equivalent gains feel good. Not metaphorically — measurably, replicably, across 19 countries.

Part II

From Human to Rational

You've seen where you fall. Now let's see what optimal looks like — and where it breaks down.

What is Expected Value?

A rational actor reduces every decision to a single number: Expected Value. Probability × Outcome, summed across all possibilities. No emotion, no framing, no sunk costs.

For Q1: 50% × $1,000 = $500. For the sure $500: 100% × $500 = $500. Identical. A rational actor is perfectly indifferent. Your brain was not.

von Neumann & Morgenstern, Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944)

The Cost of Your Biases

Each deviation from EV-maximisation has a measurable dollar cost. The chart shows your 5 decisions plotted against the rational baseline.

Each dot shows your choice against the rational baseline. Pulled left of centre — you deviated. The further the distance, the larger your irrationality premium.

Thaler (1980) — mental accounting
The Limit

Rationality has a ceiling.

Perfect EV maximisation only works in a fixed, known environment.
When the payoff depends on what someone else does — the landscape shifts. Individual optimisation isn't enough. This is where game theory begins.

Savage (1954)
Single Agent

Finding the Minimum

Before strategy, there's structure. Watch what single-agent optimisation looks like.

The Loss Landscape

Every optimisation problem has a loss landscape — a surface where the height represents how bad a solution is. The goal: find the lowest point.

The marble starts at a random position. Gravity — gradient descent — pulls it downhill. It always moves in the direction of steepest descent.

Gradient descent — Cauchy (1847) · Rumelhart et al. (1986)

Local vs Global Minimum

The landscape has valleys. Some are shallow — local minima. One is deepest — the global minimum. A naive marble gets trapped. A smarter one escapes.

This is the fundamental tension of optimisation: exploration vs exploitation. Commit too early and you're stuck. Explore forever and you never converge.

Exploration-exploitation tradeoff — foundational to reinforcement learning

Now Add Another Player

A second marble appears. The surface shifts — the minimum moves based on where the other marble is. Each agent's optimal choice depends on the other's.

Individual gradient descent no longer works. You need a new solution concept. You need Nash Equilibrium.

Nash (1950)
Two Agents

Hawk-Dove: Evolutionary Equilibrium

A mixed equilibrium. No pure strategy dominates — the population finds a precise stable ratio, regardless of where it starts.

Hawk-Dove: The Setup

Two agents compete for a resource. A Hawk always fights — high reward if they win, costly injury if they meet another Hawk. A Dove always shares or retreats — safe, but never wins against a Hawk.

Neither pure strategy dominates. There's a stable equilibrium — but it's not all-Hawk or all-Dove. It's a precise ratio. Watch the population find it.

Use the controls. Try any starting percentage. The population always converges to the same place.

Maynard Smith & Price (1973)

Watch the Population Evolve

Each round, agents compete against a random neighbour. Successful strategies spread. Costly ones retreat. The population is self-correcting — it drifts toward the equilibrium from any starting point.

This is what makes it an Evolutionarily Stable Strategy: not just a Nash equilibrium, but one that's resistant to invasion by any alternative strategy.

Maynard Smith (1982)

Part III

Game Theory in the Real World

The Ozone Problem

By the 1970s, scientists confirmed that CFCs — cheap, useful, widely manufactured — were destroying the stratospheric ozone layer. Every nation had an incentive to keep producing them. No nation had an incentive to stop unilaterally.

This is the collective action problem in its purest form. Individual rationality produces collective disaster. The Nash equilibrium is everyone keeps polluting.

Molina & Rowland (1974)

The Cooperation Signal

In 1987, 198 nations signed the Montreal Protocol — the first universally ratified treaty in UN history. They agreed to phase out ozone-depleting substances on a binding schedule.

Watch the chart. CFC production collapses. The ozone hole stabilises. By 2040 the ozone layer is expected to recover to 1980 levels. This is what successful cooperation looks like in data.

Montreal Protocol (1987)

Why Did It Work?

The Protocol succeeded because it was structured as a repeated game with verification. Binding commitments, phased schedules, and monitoring mechanisms transformed a one-shot defection trap into a cooperative equilibrium.

443 million skin cancer cases avoided. 2.3 million cancer deaths prevented. 0.5–1°C of warming avoided by mid-century. The payoff from cooperation, measured.

UNEP — 99% of ozone-depleting substances phased out
Act II — The Prisoner's Dilemma

70,000 Warheads

The most expensive Nash equilibrium in history.

The Trap

The US-Soviet arms race is the textbook Prisoner's Dilemma. Both sides would be better off with mutual disarmament. But if one disarms while the other doesn't — catastrophic vulnerability.

The dominant strategy for both is to arm. The Nash equilibrium is mutual armament. Pareto inferior. Individually rational. Collectively catastrophic.

Schelling (1960)

The Escalation

From 1945 to 1986, combined warhead counts climbed from hundreds to over 70,000. Watch the lines diverge and reconverge. Every spike has a name: Sputnik. Cuba. MIRV. SDI.

The interesting finding: both sides actually preferred mutual reduction. What sustained the race was misperception — each side believed the other preferred unilateral armament.

Plous (1993)

Communication Breaks the Trap

SALT I (1972). SALT II (1979). START I (1991). Each treaty was a credible commitment device — transforming a one-shot defection game into a repeated game with verification.

By 1991, warhead counts began declining. The mechanism wasn't trust — it was structured repeated interaction with mutual verification. Exactly what game theory prescribes.

START I (1991) — structured verification enables cooperation
Act III — The Game We're Playing Now

Human & AI

The most consequential strategic interaction of this century. Play it yourself.

AI Plays Too Rationally

When GPT-4 plays economic games, it excels at pure logic — but struggles with cooperation. Researchers found it would spot a threat and retaliate instantly, but couldn't build trust incrementally.

The same trait that makes AI a perfect calculator makes it a poor cooperator — it optimises too aggressively, too fast.

Akata et al. (2023)

But Humans Exploit AI

The dynamic runs both ways. In cross-cultural experiments, US participants cooperated with AI significantly less than with humans. They felt bad exploiting humans — not machines.

Japanese participants treated AI and humans equivalently. The asymmetry is cultural, not rational. We apply different moral frameworks to agents we perceive as non-human.

Nature Scientific Reports (2025)

Play the Game

You're now playing a Prisoner's Dilemma against a real AI. Each round you choose to Cooperate or Defect. The AI sees your history and responds.

Zimmaro et al. (2024)

What Just Happened?

Each personality you played against was a real Claude model given a different system prompt — not a scripted bot. The Mirror tracked your pattern. The Negotiator required two consecutive cooperates before trusting again. The Opportunist was prompted to build trust then defect. The Cold Strategist was told: one defection ends cooperation permanently.

Same AI. Different instructions. Completely different emergent behaviour. This is what makes the alignment problem hard — the question isn't whether AI can cooperate, it's which AI you're dealing with, and what it was told to want.

Axelrod (1984) · Akata et al. (2023)

Scoring Methodology

Consistency Score
Compares Q1 (gain frame) and Q2 (loss frame). Both questions have identical expected values. A consistent decision-maker applies the same risk attitude regardless of framing. 100% = same choice type in both frames. 0% = classic loss aversion flip. Binary by design — there is no partial consistency.
Rationality Score
Measured across Q3, Q4, and Q5 — the three questions with a clear objectively rational answer per Expected Utility Theory. Q3: Stay home (sunk cost is irrelevant to the forward decision). Q4: $150 in a year (+50% return, strictly dominant). Q5: Gamble ($60 EV > $40 certain). One point per rational answer ÷ 3.
Like Everyone Score
Compares your answers to empirically measured population baselines. Q1 left (Sure $500): 80% of people. Q2 right (Gamble): 70%. Q3 left (Go anyway): 65%. Q4 left ($100 now): 72%. Q5 left (Sure $40): 68%. One point per match with the majority choice ÷ 5.
